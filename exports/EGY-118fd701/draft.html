- C0 — Agricultural context & need (8 indicators, context, never aggregated into a score)

- C2 — Data infrastructure & DPI (12 indicators, capability, weight 30%)
- C3 — Policy, governance & safeguards (13 indicators, capability, weight 25%)
- C4 — Human & institutional capacity (13 indicators, capability, weight 20%)
- E1 — Innovation & private sector (13 indicators, ecosystem, weight 55%)
- E2 — Responsible AI & emerging tech (12 indicators, ecosystem, weight 45%)
- O1 — Inclusion, sustainability & outcomes (15 indicators, outcome, weight 100%)

47 indicators are quantitative thresholds (a number places the country on a 1–5 level); 40 are anchored capability rubrics (a written capability — a registry, a law, a coordination body — assessed clause-by-clause against anchor text per level); the remainder profile context and are reported without scoring.

HOW EVERY INDICATOR IS COLLECTED. Each collected reading carries four things or it does not enter the evidence base: the source it was taken from (a public URL), the observation year the source states, a credibility grade (A national/official exact and current, through E unusable — uncited readings are capped at E and never scored), and the maturity level the reading supports. What cannot be collected and verified becomes a named gap routed to a steward — never a guess.

THE READ-OUTS. The model reports three scores on a 1–5 scale and refuses to average them together: CMS (government capability, the 4 capability pillars weighted), EMS (the market ecosystem), and OES (outcomes and equity). A pillar below the coverage gate reports Not rated rather than pretending. Score bands: Nascent (1.0–1.8), Emerging (1.8–2.6), Established (2.6–3.4), Advanced (3.4–4.2), Transformative (4.2–5.0).

## 2. Why these conclusions matter

The Egyptian agriculture sector is material to water use, exports and rural livelihoods. Public-domain findings report that agriculture accounts for **80% of Egypt’s water consumption** (Netherlands agroberichten, n.d.; no observation year provided; **STALE**). Digital agriculture could improve input efficiency, advisory services and market access, but the evidence base is not ready to tell decision-makers how ready Egypt is, where to invest first, or what scale is justified.

These are enabling, not agricultural capability.
- **Several agricultural digital capabilities are not rated.** This includes agricultural context and need, policy and safeguards, innovation and private sector, responsible AI, and inclusion and outcomes.
- **The proposed shortlist should not be treated as an approved national priority.** It is a proposed targeting option recorded in the delivery loop.
- **Further investment decisions should be gated, not pre-committed.**

## 2. Why these conclusions matter for investment and financing

Investment and financing decisions in digital agriculture are typically built on two assumptions: that the country has a claimable maturity stage, and that the main constraints are known. For Egypt in this assessment, neither holds.

It would also allocate capital to unverified pain points while leaving the core evidence floor incomplete. That would increase the risk of financing pilot projects that cannot later be sustained, scaled, or evaluated.

The evidence position matters because it determines the **sequence of money**. In a mature or rated context, the natural first move may be to finance digital platforms, IoT deployment, or AI tools. In this context, the first move is to finance **readiness confirmation**: data validation, legal review, affordability measurement, chain-specific evidence, and the creation of conditions under which later investments can be claimed as evidence-based.

This is not a recommendation to delay all digital agriculture investment indefinitely. It is a recommendation to make the first investment in the evidence and governance conditions that the assessment itself says are missing.
